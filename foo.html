The concept you are describing is a classic behavioral economics and game theory puzzle known as the "Guess 2/3 of the average" game, which perfectly illustrates the concept of K-level reasoning (or cognitive hierarchy theory). It is also famously known as the Keynesian Beauty Contest, named after economist John Maynard Keynes, who compared stock market investing to a newspaper contest where players must choose the faces that other players will find most beautiful.

The Game Setup

Imagine a group of people are told to choose a whole number between 0 and 100. The winner is whoever guesses closest to 2/3 of the average of all the numbers guessed.

Game Theory vs. Human Psychology

If you analyze this using pure, hyper-rational game theory, the math spirals down to zero. However, when playing with real humans, you have to predict exactly how many steps ahead they are thinking. This breakdown is categorized by K-levels of reasoning:

  • Level 0: These players do not think strategically at all. They pick a number completely at random. The average of random numbers between 0 and 100 is 50.
  • Level 1: These players assume everyone else is a Level 0 player. They think: "The average will be 50, so I should guess 2/3 of 50." They guess 33.
  • Level 2: These players assume the room is full of Level 1 players. They think: "Everyone else is going to guess 33, so I need to take 2/3 of 33." They guess 22.
  • Level 3: They assume everyone else is at Level 2, calculating 2/3 of 22 to guess 15.

The Nash Equilibrium

If everyone in the room is perfectly, infinitely rational, this chain reaction continues endlessly (33 to 22 to 15 to 10 to 7...).

Eventually, the numbers bottom out. Zero is the Nash Equilibrium for this game. If absolutely everyone guesses 0, the average is 0, and 2/3 of 0 is 0. At this point, no single player can improve their position by changing their number.

How Real People Actually Play

If you actually play 0 in a real-world experiment, you will almost certainly lose.

Why? Because real human beings are not perfectly rational calculating machines. Real-world data from large-scale experiments shows a distinct behavioral pattern:

  • The Most Popular Guess: 33 (showing a huge chunk of the population stops precisely at Level 1 thinking).
  • The Second Most Popular Guess: 0 or 1 (the hyper-rational game theorists who understand the Nash Equilibrium but fail to account for human error).
  • The Winning Numbers: In large public pools, the actual average typically lands somewhere that makes the winning target fall between 11 and 22. In some massive public media experiments, the average drops even lower, bringing the winning target closer to 6.

Real-World Application: The Stock Market

This isn't just a parlor trick; it's exactly how financial markets and asset pricing function.

As Keynes originally pointed out, professional investing isn't about buying the stock you think is fundamentally worth the most. It is not even about buying what you think the average person thinks is worth the most. It is about anticipating what the average opinion expects the average opinion to be. You are trying to catch the wave just one step ahead of the crowd.
